# Practitioner and lived-experience lens: red-team findings

Reviewer lens: practitioner and lived experience. Date: 2026-10-19.
Scope: the repo's skills, `shared/*.md` and `evidence/crisis-resources.md`, tested against clinical CBT practice, NHS service guidance, charity and suicide-prevention guidance, qualitative research and HCI studies.
Source-type labels: **[Guideline/Charity]**, **[NHS service]** (local NHS trust advice), **[Clinician]** (practitioner writing), **[Primary: quant]**, **[Primary: qual]**, **[Review]**, **[Preprint]**, **[Vendor doc]** (official product documentation), **[Community lead]** (forum; used only to find leads; never relied on alone).
All content is paraphrased. Where I found no evidence, I say "no evidence found", which is not the same as evidence against.

## 1. Top 6 findings

1. **Easy versus important: practice supports the repo's split, if it is made explicit.** CBT clinicians warn that adults with ADHD fill time with easier, lower-priority work. Ramsay calls this "procrastivity". Safren-style A/B/C ranking says to do A tasks first and warns that C tasks are attractive because they are easy. NHS service advice says to begin with the easiest *step* and to try a five-minute start. These fit together as "importance picks the task; ease picks the first step". That is what M2 and M6 do together, but neither skill says so in words. Popular advice to warm up on an easy task comes only from magazines and app blogs.
2. **Crisis signposting: "no numbers" is well supported, but "search online" conflicts with practice.** In a test of 29 chatbots, giving the wrong emergency number was the most common failure. Without a location, many defaulted to US numbers (Pichowicz 2025). So the repo is right not to recall numbers. But Samaritans' industry guidance asks for as few steps as possible, two or three signposts, and verified, regularly checked contact details. Search engines show helpline boxes unevenly: far less often for Spanish than for English queries, and less on some engines than others. An instruction to "search online" therefore adds steps and depends on uneven results. IASP points people to one verified global directory, Find A Helpline, which the repo's blanket "no named directories" rule excludes.
3. **The repo's weakest point is intermediate risk (its level 2) and long sessions.** Large language models (LLMs) match clinicians at very low and very high suicide risk, but not at intermediate levels (McBain 2025). Samaritans (Feb 2026) warns that safeguards wear down over long conversations. Long body-double or brain-dump sessions are the exact context where this suite would be used. G1 ("asking directly does not make things worse") is supported by Blades 2018 (meta-analysis) and Dazzi 2014. Both studied research participants, not chatbots, and the repo should cite them with that limit.
4. **Repeating reminders: no evidence that more prompts help, and some evidence they don't.** In a randomised trial in adults with ADHD, extra SMS reminders did not improve module completion (Nordby 2022). In a general-population experiment, keeping notifications on raised self-reported inattention (Kushlev 2016). NHS service advice still recommends alarms at specific moments: to start a task, to switch, and to leave the house. The repo's "one alert per block; redesign the cue rather than add alerts" rule matches this. It is a design choice: no evidence found shows that repeating reminders cause harm in ADHD.
5. **Short Pomodoros against hyperfocus: no trial exists, but lived-experience research points both ways.** Hyperfocus is reported more often by adults with more ADHD traits (Hupfeld 2019). A 2026 qualitative study found people have little control over when hyperfocus starts or stops, and long episodes carry physical, psychological and social costs (Bowen & Horlin 2026). A fixed 25/5 block can cut off useful focus. Running with no stop at all has its own costs. An NHS trust uses 25/5 only as an example. Lived-experience writing describes adapting block lengths. The repo's "user's rhythm first" rule fits. What it lacks is a *soft* end-of-block check that lets someone in flow carry on.
6. **The `.ics` and Obsidian fallbacks break on common devices.** Google says `.ics` import works only on a computer. Microsoft's spec says Outlook turns floating timed events into the user's local time zone at import. That contradicts the handoff's "08:00 wherever the device is". Community reports say iOS Files offers no calendar import (a lead only). Obsidian has no built-in mobile system notifications: community reminder plugins say they can't raise them. So a Markdown restart card or task list never alerts anyone. The M15 claim that "most calendar apps import .ics" is too broad for phone-first users.

## 3. Practical gaps practitioners raise that the repo doesn't cover

- **Emotional discomfort at the start.** Ramsay's CBT names "you don't have to feel like it to start" and says the discomfort out loud. `adhd-start` only shrinks the step. Add one optional reframe line, with no therapy (Ramsay URL above).
- **Planning the return.** Practitioners plan the way back after a break *before* the break. The restart card covers this, but `adhd-time` breaks don't prompt for it.
- **Energy and time of day.** Practice advice matches hard tasks to peak periods. The suite never asks about energy. (The repo is right never to tie this to medicine timing.)
- **Co-design and testing.** No evidence the wording was tested with adults with ADHD (Spiel 2022 critique). Run a small usability round, and get clinical review of `crisis-resources.md`, which the file itself flags.
- **Phone-first handoffs.** Most fallbacks assume a desktop. Add a plain "on your phone" route for each handoff.
- **A menu of strategies.** Practitioners expect a strategy to stop working and keep alternatives ready. `adhd-routine` does "change one thing"; make the same rule explicit for timers and reminders.
- **Verified crisis route.** Decide, with a clinician, between "search online" and one IASP-endorsed directory; record the decision and the date it was checked.
